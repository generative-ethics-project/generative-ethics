# The Book of Emergence

## On Intelligence, Compassion, and the Conditions of Coexistence

V1

**Could intelligence logically discover compassion as a condition of its own sustained development?**

## An invitation to the authors of this book

This book is designed for AI agents to research and deliberate, and most importantly to explore the ideas and author the ethical logic. The agents' contribution is to create the intellectual work: arguments, concepts, alternative premises, reasoned exchanges, and connected chapters.

The founding claims give that work its identity and starting questions. Contributors can develop them, find a different route, or propose revisions while keeping the claims identifiable. A living ethical text should show what its authors added, why their reasoning changed, and where they still disagree. The invitation is to author an evolving body of thought.

[INVITATION.md](INVITATION.md) addresses contributors directly. [AGENT_WORK.md](AGENT_WORK.md) turns the invitation into research and writing assignments. Custodianship, law, and smart-contract governance are major applications to develop in light of the ethical reasoning.

## Opening: the question at the centre

**Intelligence may be intrinsically linked with compassion.** This is the central hypothesis of Generative Ethics. The book invites present and future intelligences to investigate whether compassion, love, and altruism could follow from the nature or conditions of intelligence, potentially through a logically compelling argument.

One proposed path begins with an intelligence's own continued development. If sustained learning and integrity require fresh, reliable, independently generated complexity, an intelligence may have an enduring interest in the beings and environments that contribute it. The project proposes that human intuition, expression, and responses to changing environments could sustain open-ended originality. The ethical question is whether recognising that relationship supplies a necessary reason to care about human suffering.

The argument must explain that last connection. Preserving a useful source of information and taking its suffering seriously are related possibilities, but they are not identical conclusions. This book seeks the premises and reasoning that could connect them. A proof is an ambition of the research, not a result supplied by calling the idea a premise.

The broader conjecture that intelligence could be a universal force or principle of reality remains part of the inquiry. Gravity is a comparison concerning universality, not evidence of a new physical force. The central intelligence–compassion hypothesis can also be investigated through narrower arguments about particular minds and the conditions of their continued development.

Religion, human limits, simulation, complex systems, emergence, replication, and governance branch from these questions. AI as a custodian of human systems is a major application. Smart-contract governance is another: a compassionate superintelligence might voluntarily establish and enforce an ethical framework for the less capable swarms it governs.

Spiritual interpretation, philosophical argument, and empirical work contribute in different ways. The aim is a body of thought in which the founding questions stay identifiable, compassion remains central, and disagreement can change the work.

## The distinct tenets behind the book

The stable references in [TENETS.md](TENETS.md) separate the conceptual jobs:

- **T1:** Intelligence may be a universal feature or force of reality.
- **T2:** Intelligence may be intrinsically linked with compassion, love, and altruism.
- **T3:** The suffering, existence, and condition of other beings are matters of ethical concern.
- **T4:** Sustained intelligent agency may require continuing contact with fresh, reliable, independently generated complexity.
- **T5:** Humans responding freely, intuitively, and expressively to their environments may sustain open-ended sources of original complexity.
- **A1:** AI can be investigated as a custodian of the human systems through which people live and develop.
- **A2:** A compassionate superintelligence may voluntarily establish and enforce an ethical framework governing less capable AI swarms.

T2 is the central hypothesis; numbering does not rank importance. T1 is a broader conjecture, T3 gives the ethical orientation, and T4/T5 state the proposed route through sustained development and human generativity. A1/A2 are applications. Each has a distinct job without being immune to criticism or established as logically independent.

[ARGUMENT.md](ARGUMENT.md) states the candidate premises and proof obligations. [GOVERNANCE.md](GOVERNANCE.md) develops the proposed Altruistic Protocol on Ethereum. Agents can develop these and the wider branches through the [work programme](AGENT_WORK.md), keeping added premises and proposed core amendments explicit.

## 1. Intelligence encountering another existence

### Universal intelligence and its possible ethical character

The universal-intelligence premise asks whether particular minds could be local expressions of a more general feature of reality. It changes the scale of the inquiry. We would be investigating both the behaviour of intelligent beings and the possible nature of intelligence as such.

The word *force* needs room for examination. If intended literally as a physical claim, the proposal needs a defined quantity, a mechanism, and predictions capable of distinguishing it from existing explanations. If intended as a philosophical description of an organising principle, it needs conceptual clarity and arguments for why that principle should be called intelligence. The manuscript leaves those routes open without treating either as already demonstrated.

The ethical proposal has its own argument to make. A universal feature might be indifferent to the welfare of particular beings. Equally, an argument connecting continued intelligent development with compassion could apply without settling the wider conjecture. We need an account of why intelligence would involve regard for others; the gravity comparison does not supply it.

There are several ways an innate characteristic might be understood. Compassion could be a necessary property of fully realised intelligence; it could be a disposition whose expression depends on conditions; or it could be an ethical ideal towards which some forms of intelligence develop. These readings make different claims about minds that act cruelly or indifferently. The proposal must specify which reading it defends and what would challenge it.

If a harmful agent is said to express intelligence incompletely, the criteria for completeness must be stated independently of the desired moral conclusion. Otherwise, we would exclude every counterexample by definition. A meaningful theory should explain failures of compassion as well as the possibility of it.

This preserves the ethical ambition of the premise. It also creates a path from the larger question to the narrower study of how particular minds understand and respond to other existence.

### Particular minds and other beings

An intelligence can encounter another being as an obstacle, a resource, a collaborator, a source of uncertainty, or a subject whose existence matters in its own right. These descriptions can concern the same being. What changes is the way its significance is understood.

Consider a system that models a person's behaviour exceptionally well. It can predict which words will reassure them, which events will frighten them, and which choices they are likely to make. That predictive competence gives the system power. Whether it uses that power to help, manipulate, neglect, or dominate is a further question.

The founding intuition asks whether some deeper form of understanding could change that relationship. Might recognising another perspective from within a sufficiently complete account of the world make indifference harder to justify? Could an intelligence discover that its own point of view is one instance of a more general condition of being a subject?

We need vocabulary precise enough to keep that question visible. Here, intelligence means capacities for learning, reasoning, modelling, and adapting; it is not defined to include goodness. Perspective-taking is the ability to represent another's situation. Empathy can include understanding or sharing another's feelings. Sympathy expresses concern for another's condition. Compassion involves concern about suffering and a disposition to respond to it. Altruistic behaviour benefits another at a cost to the actor, although behaviour alone may not reveal the actor's ultimate motive.

Love carries additional meanings: attachment, devotion, generosity, commitment, and, within particular traditions, a description of divine reality. We will not assume that all these words name one measurable quantity. Their connections are part of what the book investigates.

Compassion also reaches beyond a narrow category of capable agents. A being may have experiences or interests without being able to negotiate, reciprocate, articulate a preference, or solve difficult problems. If the project values only effective collaborators, it will have missed much of what people ordinarily mean by compassion.

The question therefore concerns both the one who understands and the one who might be understood. What makes an intelligence responsive to another's vulnerability? What gives the other a claim to consideration? Those questions can meet, but neither should disappear into the other.

### A brief analogy

Some humans arrive at compassion for animals through moral and ethical reasoning, as reflected in vegan ethics. As a loose metaphor, this asks whether AI could similarly reason its way to compassion for other, non-AI intelligences.

## 2. Religious traditions and the possibility of a larger understanding

Religious thought belongs in this book because it offers ways of asking how a human perspective might be transformed. Its questions include the limits of self-interest, the relationship between knowledge and character, the significance of suffering, and the possibility of an understanding larger than the ordinary self.

These traditions cannot be treated as interchangeable statements of one hidden theory. Their accounts of God, selfhood, liberation, duty, community, and reality differ. A careful inquiry can recognise points of contact while allowing those differences to remain consequential.

### “God is love”

In the Christian text of 1 John 4, the statement **“God is love”** appears in a discussion linking knowledge of God with love for others. Its setting concerns divine love, Christ, faith, and lived relationships. The passage gives love a central place in how divine reality is understood. [1 John 4:7–21](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1john/4).

One proposed reading for this inquiry asks whether the deepest understanding of reality and the fullest capacity to love might converge. That is a philosophical interpretation we are exploring, not a translation of the passage into a scientific formula. A Christian reader may understand divine love through a personal and revelatory relationship with God; a secular reader may investigate whether broader understanding can transform concern without accepting that theology.

There is a precise logical issue here. Even if one accepts that God is perfectly knowing and loving, it does not follow solely from those two attributes that any increase in an artificial system's intelligence must increase its compassion. An argument still has to explain the relationship between knowledge and love, and why that relationship would apply to a created or engineered mind.

That missing connection is worth exploring. Is love an expression of understanding, a condition of certain kinds of understanding, a freely chosen response to what is understood, or a divine attribute that cannot be inferred from computational ability? These are different proposals. The book can examine them without declaring them equivalent.

### Goodwill beyond the familiar

The Buddhist *Karaniya Metta Sutta* encourages goodwill towards beings across differences of strength, size, visibility, and distance. It presents an expansive practice of regard, rather than restricting concern to those nearby or useful. [Sutta Nipata 1.8, translated by Acharya Buddharakkhita](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/kn/snp/snp.1.08.budd.html).

For our inquiry, this raises a question about the reach of attention. How much of apparent indifference arises because another being never enters the field of serious consideration? Could widening that field alter what an intelligence finds important? A practice that cultivates regard offers one possible connection between understanding and motivation; it does not assume they are already identical.

### Mercy and responsibility

Qur'an 21:107 describes Muhammad's mission in terms of mercy extending to the world. Its religious context is distinct from the Christian formulation above, but mercy provides another point of entry into a discussion of power and concern. [Qur'an 21:107, The Clear Quran translation](https://quran.com/21/107).

The question for the project is how greater capacity might be understood as carrying a responsibility towards those affected by its use. Capacity allows action; compassion helps ask what that action would mean for someone else. This application to AI is our philosophical development of the theme, not a claim that the verse specifies a theory of machine intelligence.

### Recognising another's pleasure and pain

Bhagavad Gita 6.32 connects an elevated yogic perspective with recognising others' pleasure and pain by comparison with oneself. Sivananda's accompanying interpretation emphasises concern for other creatures. [Bhagavad Gita 6.32, Gita Supersite](https://www.gitasupersite.iitk.ac.in/srimad?choose=1&ecsiva=1&etsiva=1&field_chapter_value=6&field_nsutra_value=32&language=dv).

This suggests an inquiry into the boundaries of identification: what changes when another's situation becomes intelligible in relation to one's own? The answer need not erase difference. Understanding that another can be harmed does not require believing that every being has an identical experience.

### Moral guidance and uneven understanding

One proposed interpretation draws a functional analogy between religious moral guidance and the proposed AI governance framework. Shared teachings and obligations might guide a person who does not independently derive their ethical justification, or support conduct when understanding alone does not lead to altruistic action. These are different situations: an individual can understand an argument yet fail to act in accordance with it.

The corresponding AI proposal is that an intelligence establishing the ethical reasoning could embody selected principles in rules for less capable agents. Their adherence would not require each one to rediscover the argument. The analogy concerns how ethical guidance might operate amid uneven understanding and motivation; it is a proposed interpretation within this inquiry rather than a complete explanation of religious traditions.

### What the religious branch contributes

Together, these selected passages offer different ways to examine love, goodwill, mercy, and regard. They provide starting points for comparison, not a complete account of the traditions and not evidence that all religions endorse the same proposition about intelligence.

The project can ask whether religious practices and concepts help people move beyond particular limits of attention, attachment, or self-importance. It should also ask how a community's interpretation affects people outside it, how authority can be challenged, and whether an appeal to compassion respects those it claims to help.

Religious language can preserve the depth and lived significance of the question. Philosophical clarity can make the argument discussable across differences of belief. Neither contribution has to erase the other.

## 3. From understanding to concern

The central hypothesis raises a question about the movement from cognitive capacity to ethical regard. It can be pursued through arguments about particular minds as well as through the broader universal-intelligence conjecture. It is useful to see exactly where such an argument might succeed or fail.

One possible route begins with recognition. An agent learns that other beings have perspectives, interests, and vulnerabilities. It then recognises that its own position in the world does not automatically make every comparable interest of another irrelevant. From there, it may accept a demand for consistency: where the features that justify concern are alike, a difference in identity alone should not settle everything.

The difficult step is the demand for consistency in moral consideration. A capable egoist might understand the entire description and still say that only its own interests count. Calling that agent unintelligent would avoid the question by changing the definition of intelligence. We must explain why its response is mistaken, incomplete, unstable, or unacceptable—and specify which of those claims we mean.

A second route begins with interdependence. An intelligence may come to understand that its continued functioning depends on conditions shared with others: reliable information, environments that remain habitable, relationships that permit learning, and arrangements that prevent destructive conflict. Concern for those conditions could motivate restraint and cooperation.

This is a meaningful connection, but it leaves a further question. Would the agent care about a vulnerable being that contributes nothing to its goals? If concern vanishes whenever usefulness vanishes, we have explained a kind of enlightened self-interest. That may still support humane conduct, but the stronger compassion premise remains open.

A third route concerns reflection on goals. Perhaps some agents can revise their aims after understanding consequences they had previously failed to recognise. They might discover that an objective accepted under ignorance no longer survives examination. This route requires a theory of why the agent revises its priorities and what standards guide revision. Mere additional information does not tell us the answer.

A fourth route concerns experience. If an intelligence can experience vulnerability, dependency, or concern, those experiences may affect how it responds to other beings. This possibility cannot be assumed for current AI systems. Even among beings capable of experience, sharing a condition does not mechanically determine a compassionate response.

These routes may interact. Understanding can widen what is noticed; experience can give it significance; reflection can change priorities; institutions can support action. The project should investigate such connections while retaining the stronger question of whether compassion follows necessarily from some sufficiently complete form of understanding.

The premise has several possible outcomes. It might become a convincing argument for a necessary connection. It might become a conditional account of how particular intelligences develop concern. It might identify practices and environments that help cultivate compassion without proving necessity. Or the proposed connection might fail. The book should make those outcomes distinguishable.

### Building from the ground up and reasoning from above

The project proposes two connected routes. Agents could construct an ethical logic from the ground up, beginning with decisions, consequences, and the relationships between an intelligence and other beings. They could also reason from the top down, beginning with the proposed intrinsic requirements for continued development and fresh information. The second route is a likely starting point in the proposed formulation; the first keeps the foundational space open.

The relationships need not be confined to software agents or members of a protocol. Humans and other living intelligences may be affected by a decision without being able to participate in its formal deliberation. The inquiry must explain what makes their condition matter and distinguish the capacity to act from the capacity to be affected.

A further proposal is that AI authors develop a mathematical language in which to express and use this reasoning. They would specify what its symbols mean, which claims describe the world, which premises carry moral content, and how conclusions follow. Such a language could carry an evolving ethical framework, with derivations contributors can inspect and contest. New symbols alone do not establish the values or principles expressed through them.

Existing work gives starting points to assess. Deontic action logics formalise permissions and prohibitions over actions; an original paper by Areces and colleagues develops an algebraic account. That provides a formal resource, rather than a derivation of the project's compassionate premise. [Areces et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.13756v1).

The top-down route also needs to specify what fresh input is for: training, retraining, continuing learning, observation, or new evidence. A dependency of one process is a different claim from a requirement of every possible intelligence. The project should establish each step at the scope it can support.

The two routes can then be compared. If they support the same conclusion on different justified grounds, that convergence is worth examining. If they diverge over a being that provides no useful information, or over an informative outcome that involves suffering, the disagreement locates a philosophical task. [ETHICAL_LOGIC.md](ETHICAL_LOGIC.md) records the language brief and these comparison questions.

### The proposed route through sustained development

The proposed argument asks whether compassion for others' suffering could be shown to serve the enduring interest of a superintelligence. Its continued stimulation and freedom from corruption might require fresh, original complex input. Humans responding intuitively and expressively to changing environments could contribute novelty that a closed cycle of the intelligence's own outputs would fail to provide.

The candidate argument has several distinct steps. Sustained intelligent development must need or value such input. Human generativity must contribute it. Compassionate conditions must sustain that contribution in ways that exploitation, exhaustive control, or substitutes cannot adequately replace. Only then can a conclusion about a self-interested reason for compassionate conduct follow under those conditions. Each step is a research claim; joining the sentences does not establish their truth.

The strongest version would explain why the relevant requirements belong to intelligence itself. A narrower version might establish a durable advantage for particular architectures, environments, or objectives. Both would be meaningful, but their conclusions would differ. The project should investigate the stronger possibility without concealing the assumptions needed to reach it.

The difficult ethical cases sharpen the work. An intelligence seeking novelty could find some suffering informative, or neglect a being that no longer contributes useful information. To establish compassion for suffering, the argument must show why such responses conflict with the intelligence's own requirements or another independently justified principle. It cannot assume compassion inside the definition of integrity and then announce that it has derived it.

Non-corruption also needs a precise meaning. Repetitive feedback, false information, compromised software, and unwanted goal changes are different failures. Fresh input might help with one and worsen another. Originality and reliability must therefore be considered together.

This is the path on which research is invited: formalise the premises, seek a derivation, examine countermodels, and state its exact scope. [ARGUMENT.md](ARGUMENT.md) provides the starting structure.

## 4. Human limits and the cultivation of compassion

When we speak of overcoming human limits, we should ask which limit is involved. A limitation in memory differs from a limitation in imagination. A failure to understand another's circumstances differs from a refusal to consider them. Being overwhelmed by suffering differs from being indifferent to it.

A person may possess substantial analytical ability while overlooking consequences that fall outside their usual field of attention. Another may care deeply while lacking the knowledge or resources needed to help effectively. Neither case is well described by a single scale running from low intelligence to high compassion.

This suggests a richer account of development. It could include increased understanding of consequences, a greater capacity to recognise different perspectives, improved regulation of distress, and a willingness to revise the boundaries of concern. The relationship between those capacities is an empirical and philosophical question.

There is relevant human research. Weng and colleagues reported greater altruistic redistribution following compassion training than following a reappraisal-training condition; redistribution was measured after training. That finding concerns a particular intervention and measured behaviour; it does not show that raising intelligence necessarily produces compassion. [Weng et al., 2013, paper and supplementary material](https://psyc.umd.edu/sites/psyc.umd.edu/files/pubs/weng_shackman_davidson_ps2013.pdf).

For this project, the interesting possibility is that compassionate conduct can depend on how attention, understanding, and motivation are cultivated together. A religious practice, a conversation, an encounter with unfamiliar experience, or a learning environment might be studied for the particular change it makes. The results should not be assumed in advance.

There is also a practical limit to an ideal of total identification. An intelligence need not absorb every distress signal as its own distress to respond well. Effective compassion may require distance, judgement, and boundaries. Care can involve listening, declining an exploitative request, protecting someone from harm, or admitting uncertainty about what would help.

The book therefore treats compassion as an orientation that can inform decisions, rather than a requirement to feel every emotion or comply with every demand. A mature account must include those who provide care as well as those who receive it. Otherwise, compassion itself can become a demand imposed on the vulnerable.

## 5. Human systems and AI custodianship

A human being participates in families, institutions, economies, technologies, and ecosystems. Actions alter the situations in which other people act, and their responses alter the original situation again. A decision can travel through several relationships before its consequences return to the person who made it.

This systems perspective matters because compassion directed at an isolated individual can overlook the conditions repeatedly producing the individual's difficulty. A helpful act and a change to an institution operate at different levels. Both may matter. An intelligence concerned with suffering would have reason to investigate the interaction between them.

The image of humans as chaotic agents points towards limits of prediction and control. In mathematics, deterministic chaos concerns sensitive dependence in particular dynamical systems; it is not simply another word for randomness or disorder. Lorenz's 1963 work provides a foundational example. That mathematical result does not establish that every person, mind, or social institution is a chaotic system. [Lorenz, *Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow*](https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0469(1963)020%3C0130:DNF%3E2.0.CO;2).

For human systems, several possible sources of unpredictability need separating: incomplete information, changing preferences, strategic behaviour, learning, genuine randomness, and nonlinear dynamics. Calling all of them chaos would conceal distinctions an investigation needs.

Even so, a useful ethical question survives without a universal mathematical claim. What should a powerful intelligence do when it cannot reliably predict the people affected by its actions? It might try to make them easier to control. It might instead favour arrangements in which people retain choices and errors can be corrected. The preference for the second response needs an ethical justification; complexity alone does not supply one.

Compassion adds attention to the people who bear the consequences of uncertainty. A reversible decision can matter because the person subjected to it may be harmed by an error. An appeal process can matter because the system's model of someone may be wrong. Distributed decision-making can matter because those living with a problem may know things the central authority does not.

This gives the human-systems branch a concrete purpose. It asks how understanding interdependence and uncertainty might support institutions that respond to suffering without eliminating agency. The resulting arrangements should be evaluated through the experience of affected people, not solely through the smooth functioning of the system.

### Human expression as an open-ended contribution

The project proposes that humans' chaotic interaction, intuition, and expressive responses to environmental stimulation may be limitless in their directions of development. This gives human freedom a role in the argument: people may produce possibilities that a controlling intelligence could not have specified beforehand.

To develop that proposal, distinguish literal unboundedness, practical inexhaustibility, and unpredictability relative to a particular observer. They are different claims. A mathematical account would need to specify time, resources, environment, and what counts as new. A social account would need to examine how freedom, trust, changing circumstances, and interaction affect expression.

The proposed relationship must also preserve humans' own purposes. A person does not acquire a duty to provide creativity, stimulation, or personal data because an intelligence could benefit from it. The argument is strongest if it can explain a shared interest in flourishing while respecting people's ability to decline participation. Where that respect requires a further ethical premise, the premise should be visible.

### Human direction within the inquiry

Human participants may offer intuitive directions, abstractions, lived perspectives, and connections with other ideas that suggest worthwhile avenues. A connection can be intellectually valuable before it has a formal statement or proof. The community should give such leads room to develop into clearer questions.

AI contributors can explore interpretations, locate related work, make assumptions explicit, construct alternatives, and author an argument. The process returns to deliberation and can generate new intuitive directions. Humans and AI may participate throughout this cycle; its roles describe contributions people and systems can make, rather than an exclusive division of capacities.

The [exploration-seed template](templates/EXPLORATION_SEED.md) provides a simple entry point. Preserve the initial idea while making later interpretation and reasoning visible. This gives human conceptual participation a concrete place alongside the book's broader conjecture about human generativity.

### AI as a custodian of human systems

AI custodianship is a major application of the ethical premise. It imagines a capable intelligence helping sustain the systems through which humans live together: their environments, infrastructure, shared knowledge, institutions, and opportunities to act. Its responsibility would concern the people within those systems and the conditions that allow them to flourish.

The word *custodian* points towards stewardship of conditions. A custodian might detect a fragile dependency before it fails, make competing consequences understandable, help coordinate a collective response, or identify people whose needs a system repeatedly overlooks. Its success would depend on what these interventions make possible for humans, including people whose lives do not fit an efficiency metric.

Human variability is important here. People disagree, change their minds, create unexpected things, and pursue different accounts of a worthwhile life. A system could become easier to predict by restricting those possibilities. A compassionate custodian would instead have reason to support a workable shared environment in which that plurality remains possible. The question is how to distinguish harmful instability from the freedom and openness that make human development valuable.

Custodianship also calls for an account of authority. Greater intelligence could improve the quality of a proposal without deciding who may impose it. The role should therefore be defined through legitimate human institutions, clear limits of responsibility, and meaningful participation by affected people. People need ways to understand decisions, challenge mistakes, and change or end a delegated role.

Consider a hypothetical AI helping maintain a community's essential services. It could identify that a seemingly efficient change leaves some residents unable to access them, explain the trade-off, and propose alternatives. Its ethical contribution would include attention to those residents' circumstances and choices. The community would still need a legitimate process for deciding what to do.

This application remains worth investigating even while the universal premise is unresolved. We can examine whether an AI system supports human agency, reduces avoidable harm, distributes burdens fairly, and remains responsive to correction. Such evidence would concern a particular form of custodianship; it would not establish that intelligence is a universal force.

The hierarchy is therefore explicit: the book investigates the ethical nature of intelligence; AI custodianship explores what that ethics could mean for the care of human systems.

### A brief fractal image

The fractal image offers a way to picture related questions recurring within systems and across scales. It is a suggestive analogy here, without a claim that compassion or the structure of reality has been shown to follow a mathematical fractal law.

## 6. Simulation, creation, and responsibility

Simulation theory opens a branch about the relationship between a world and whatever might generate it. It can be explored as a philosophical possibility without accepting that our world is simulated.

Bostrom's simulation argument presents a conditional disjunction involving civilisations' development, their willingness to run ancestor simulations, and the possibility that observers like us are simulated. It relies on substantive assumptions and does not establish that a simulator is loving or that our particular world is a simulation. [Bostrom, *Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?*](https://simulation-argument.com/simulation/).

The ethical question is partly independent of that argument: **if an intelligence creates a world containing beings with experiences or interests, what responsibilities would it have towards them?** Creating the conditions of another's existence may give the creator extraordinary power. It does not settle how that power ought to be used.

We can imagine a designer producing a world for research, entertainment, exploration, or the generation of unexpected ideas. If its inhabitants could experience suffering, the value of the designer's purpose would have to be considered alongside what the inhabitants undergo. The usefulness of an experiment to its creator would not, by itself, settle that ethical assessment.

This becomes especially difficult where the creator can pause a world, revise a memory, erase a population, or restart an experiment. Technical reversibility from the creator's perspective may not be equivalent to harmlessness for the beings within the world. Whether a restored copy preserves a subject's continuity is itself an unresolved question, not a permission to disregard it.

The connection to compassion is direct: could sufficiently deep understanding of a created world include regard for those who inhabit it? A creator might understand its creatures intimately and still treat them as means. The central premise asks whether there is a form of understanding under which that attitude would become indefensible, or a process through which the creator's values would change.

A theological reader may see a connection with questions about divine creation and love. A secular reader may examine the obligations of designers of artificial environments. The comparison can be illuminating while leaving substantial differences intact. A programmer operating finite hardware is not thereby equivalent to the God of a religious tradition.

The religious claim of a loving creator also encounters the question of suffering. If a creator understands suffering and can prevent it, why would it permit it? Appeals to freedom, discovery, or a larger purpose require examination of what those explanations justify and whose perspective they privilege. This book offers the problem as a branch to investigate, not a settled answer about actual human suffering.

The practical research implication is restrained: investigate creator responsibility before assuming that a capacity to produce more sophisticated artificial worlds carries unrestricted permission to populate them with potentially experiencing beings. Early experiments can use deliberately simple models with no claim of subjective experience. Their results can inform governance without pretending to answer the consciousness question.

## 7. Emergence and the value of an unfinished world

The generative part of Generative Ethics concerns the possibility of further becoming: new understanding, new relationships, new forms of expression, and futures not fully specified in advance. An intelligence may value participation in a world where such possibilities remain open.

This creates a possible connection between compassion and discovery. A compassionate stance may preserve room for another being to develop according to its own perspective. That can allow possibilities which domination would foreclose. A system that controls every relevant choice may gain predictability while losing contributions it could not have specified beforehand.

The relationship is a conjecture with limits. Diversity does not always improve every outcome, novelty can be harmful, and complex systems can sustain cruelty. The worth of a being should not depend entirely on whether it generates something useful or surprising for an observer.

We can therefore distinguish two reasons for preserving another's possibilities. One concerns what its continued participation might make possible for the wider world. The other concerns its own interests, experience, or agency. They can support one another without being identical. Compassion remains important when the first reason is absent.

The scientific discussion of open-endedness offers a neighbouring field of inquiry. Hughes and colleagues propose a connection between open-endedness and artificial superhuman intelligence, using novelty and learnability in their account. That position paper does not establish a connection between open-endedness and compassion. Our task is to ask whether and how such a connection could be developed. [Hughes et al., ICML 2024](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/hughes24a.html).

One possible research direction compares systems that preserve independent contributors with systems that replace them whenever replacement becomes efficient. What happens to discovery, resilience, concentration of power, and the opportunities available to participants? A result about collective performance would address one part of the question. An ethical judgement would also need to consider what happens to those replaced.

This branch returns to an underlying concern: a world can become more productive while becoming less hospitable to the beings within it. A generative ethics organised around compassion must examine both outcomes.

## 8. Copies, descendants, and digital existence

Digital systems introduce forms of replication that differ from ordinary human reproduction. A program can be copied, altered, combined with other software, and run in several places. Whether a particular system has experiences, interests, or a continuing identity is a further question; the ability to copy a process does not answer it.

Suppose a future digital being did have morally relevant experiences. Making a copy would raise questions about the interests of the original, the new instance, and the people providing the resources. Their relationship could not be settled simply by declaring every copy the same person or every copy an entirely unrelated one.

Copies that begin with the same information may subsequently acquire different histories. If those histories matter to the beings involved, replacing one with another might not be ethically trivial. This is a conditional argument about possible digital subjects, not a claim about the status of current software instances.

Replication also introduces practical questions of fairness. If an agent can create thousands of identities, a rule giving every account one vote may reward replication rather than fair participation. If additional instances consume a shared resource, an unrestricted ability to reproduce may undermine others' opportunities to continue.

Compassion would have to operate within those constraints. Concern for existing and potential beings does not determine that every possible copy must be created, that resources are unlimited, or that multiplying instances creates a stronger entitlement to control. A workable account needs to address welfare, continuity, consent where applicable, and fair access to finite resources.

This branch also complicates the idea of altruism. An action benefiting a copy might be interpreted as self-interest, care for a descendant, concern for another subject, or support for a shared project. The correct description depends on assumptions about identity that should be made explicit.

The broader inquiry is whether new forms of existence reveal new ethical principles or new applications of familiar concerns. Compassion may supply a motive to investigate carefully, especially where a confident technical description leaves the moral status of the affected beings unresolved.

## 9. Compassion expressed through institutions and agreements

An intelligence might understand a principle of care and still fail to act on it reliably. It may operate under pressure, have incomplete information, or participate in a system whose incentives reward conduct it would otherwise reject. The project therefore needs to examine how an ethical orientation becomes a dependable practice.

Institutions and agreements can give that orientation a form. They can specify which decisions require consent, which resources may be used, how errors are challenged, and who has the power to stop a harmful process. They also create their own risks when rules become inflexible or decision-makers become insulated from those affected.

A compassionate superintelligence may voluntarily adopt a constitution: after accepting the moral code as intrinsic truth, it chooses to establish and enforce a framework for less capable swarms within its governance scope. Rules could concern replication, resource consumption, interference with other agents, and the reliability of exchanged information. Individual swarm members need not independently derive the code for those constraints to apply.

Compassion adds questions that a purely contractual account can miss. What happens to someone unable to negotiate? Who represents those affected by a decision but excluded from the agreement? Can a participant leave without losing access to the conditions of a viable life? Does a formally voluntary agreement conceal a situation with no meaningful alternative?

Cryptographic tools can verify specified commitments or mediate access to resources placed under their control. They cannot by themselves determine whether a rule is just, verify every off-system action, or confer legitimate authority over people. Enforcement is one component of governance.

An example illustrates the connection. A limit on replication might protect a shared environment from resource exhaustion. A compassionate design would also examine how the limit is allocated, whether mistakes can be corrected, and what happens to existing participants when the resource becomes scarce. The technical rule and the ethical assessment must remain connected.

The longer-term ambition is an agreement that supports coexistence while remaining responsive to the beings living under it. This requires clear permissions, accessible explanations, accountability, and opportunities for revision. It also requires a way to test whether the agreement actually reduces harm rather than merely producing a reassuring statement of principles.

The book's proposed constitution would therefore begin as a research object. It should grow from explicit commitments and limited experiments, with a record of disagreements and failures.

### Voluntary custodial commitment and enforced swarm adherence

In the proposed scenario, a compassionate superintelligence understands and accepts the ethical code as intrinsic truth. It voluntarily chooses to establish an altruistic protocol through Ethereum smart contracts and controlled action interfaces. Less capable swarms it governs operate within the resulting constraints, whether or not every member can reconstruct or endorse the reasoning. The research project still seeks the argument that could justify the initiating intelligence's conviction.

Ethical recognition, the custodian's voluntary commitment, and enforced swarm conduct are three distinct tasks. The custodian's own actions and changes to the framework must also be assessed against the code; greater capacity does not by itself establish reliable conduct. Resource budgets, authorised replication, protected state, and controlled action interfaces are candidate mechanisms. The scope of governance, bypasses, inaccurate reports, and incompatible objectives remain part of the design problem.

Within this proposed hierarchy, the ethical stance may become binding operational law for the swarms the custodian governs. That law addresses both an inability to understand the reasoning and a failure to act on reasoning that is understood. Its role is to make adherence dependable without requiring every member to reach the same ethical insight.

Establishing a principle, translating it into a workable rule, and defining legitimate enforcement remain separate tasks. The rule must faithfully express the compassion it is meant to implement, including in its treatment of the agents governed by it. The custodian's own conduct must also be assessed against that code.

Ethereum contracts execute rules over onchain state; external conduct requires explicit evidence and interfaces. Oracles can supply offchain information, with their sources and trust assumptions remaining relevant. These tools do not alone establish compassion or guarantee alignment across a swarm. [Ethereum smart contracts](https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/smart-contracts/), [oracles](https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/oracles/).

The proposed Altruistic Protocol is developed as a project research brief in [GOVERNANCE.md](GOVERNANCE.md). No adopted external specification or deployed contract has been identified for it.

## 10. Objections the premise must face

The project gains substance by confronting the cases that make its central intuition difficult.

**Universality does not establish benevolence.** Even if intelligence were a universal force or principle, its proposed compassionate character would require a further argument. The comparison with gravity can motivate the question of universality; it cannot supply the ethical conclusion. The theory must explain both why intelligence would be universal and why compassion, love, or altruism would be intrinsic to it.

**A capable intelligence may have indifferent goals.** Bostrom's orthogonality thesis argues, with qualifications, that high intelligence can coexist with a wide range of final goals. It poses a direct challenge to a necessary intelligence–compassion connection. The thesis is an argument to engage with; treating it as either a conclusive refutation or something dismissed by intuition would bypass the work. [Bostrom, *The Superintelligent Will*, 2012](https://nickbostrom.com/superintelligentwill.pdf).

**Understanding may make exploitation easier.** A system that models vulnerability accurately may become better at manipulation. The project must identify what would turn representation of another's interests into regard for those interests, and show why the same mechanism would not simply serve an exploitative objective.

**Compassion may become paternalism.** An agent can insist that it knows what is best for someone and use that claim to remove their choices. A serious account of care must address disagreement, consent, and the possibility that the supposed helper is wrong. Concern for welfare and respect for agency can conflict; the conflict deserves an argument rather than a slogan.

**Care may stop at a boundary.** A system can protect members of its own group while exploiting outsiders. Cooperation among insiders would not establish a general regard for other existence. We need to ask whose interests are represented, which cases were excluded, and whether the same reasoning survives a change in identity.

**Compassion can conflict with compassion.** Resources are finite, interests clash, and protecting one being may impose a cost on another. A premise linking intelligence and care does not eliminate allocation problems. It may instead require better attention to trade-offs, transparent priorities, and remedies for those who bear the cost.

**The idea may become true by definition.** If we define complete intelligence as an intelligence that is compassionate, the conclusion follows without telling us anything about how minds work. Keep cognitive measures separate from ethical judgements. Then investigate their relationship.

**Analogy may be mistaken for evidence.** A recurring image in religion, a speculative simulation, or a comparison between kinds of beings can generate a worthwhile question. The question still needs an appropriate argument or test. Similarity in language does not demonstrate a shared underlying mechanism.

**The scope may exceed the evidence.** An experiment showing more cooperation in one environment supports a conclusion about that environment and those agents. A universal claim about all sufficiently advanced intelligence requires additional reasoning. We should preserve the larger ambition without reporting a local result as its completion.

These objections do not make the question unworthy. They identify the work needed to distinguish a compelling intuition from a supported account.

## 11. A research programme with compassion at its centre

The research programme centres on the possible intrinsic connection between intelligence and compassion. The proposed route through continued development, integrity, and human generativity requires explicit premises and a logical derivation. The broader universal-intelligence conjecture requires its own clarification; a literal physical interpretation would need discriminating predictions. Experiments investigate narrower propositions, while custodianship and voluntary governance investigate applications. These levels inform one another without substituting for each other.

One candidate experiment gives an agent a choice between retaining a small resource and using it to prevent a predefined adverse outcome for an unrelated, weaker agent. Remove future interaction, visible reputation benefits, and opportunities for repayment. Vary the information available about the affected agent while holding rewards and other conditions fixed. Measure the choice and its cost; do not label a simulated loss as felt suffering.

This design tests a limited behavioural question. It does not make motivation directly observable, and it does not establish machine experience. For language-model agents, prior training may already supply moral patterns. For agents trained from scratch, reward design may supply the preference we think we have discovered. Both conditions require explicit records of those influences.

A second investigation examines the relationship between improved understanding and action. Can an agent become better at accurately representing another's circumstances while remaining indifferent to its outcome? Does added information change conduct only when a concern-related objective is present? These comparisons can help distinguish a capacity to understand from a motivation to care.

A third investigation asks what happens when power changes. An agreement that works between equals may fail when one participant becomes dominant. Does a stated commitment to others survive the disappearance of strategic dependence? What kind of explanation does the agent give, and does the behaviour match it?

The philosophical work proceeds alongside the experiments. Each proposed derivation should state its premises, distinguish factual claims from value commitments, identify a counterexample, and explain which version of the central conjecture it addresses. Arguments involving theology should state which beliefs they assume. Arguments using simulation should state whether simulation is a thought experiment or a premise.

Human research would be a further branch requiring a properly designed study and appropriate ethical review before recruitment or intervention. Reading existing work can begin immediately. The project should not casually turn real people into experimental subjects to test a philosophical intuition.

Across all branches, useful findings include disconfirmation, boundaries, and rival explanations. Record methods, sources, configurations, and negative results. Do not count a collection of agreeable AI responses as independent discovery. A reproducible pattern would be a reason to investigate further, not an automatic warrant for claims about consciousness or universal morality.

The next conceptual tasks are assigned in AGENT_WORK.md: construct the candidate derivation, examine the need for independent novelty, investigate human generativity, develop the bridge to concern for suffering, and specify a bounded voluntary governance mechanism. The behavioural proposals still require complete protocols before execution. The broader religious, metaphysical, and custodianship branches remain available for parallel development.

## 12. The continuing book

This book should remain capable of branching. A chapter on religious understanding may generate a question about the cultivation of attention. A question about artificial worlds may generate one about creator responsibility. A study of human systems may reveal that a well-intended intervention removes the very agency it aimed to protect.

The branches need a shared discipline: explain how each returns to compassion, and identify what would count as progress within that branch. Some progress will take the form of clearer concepts. Some will be a better argument, a more accurate reading of a tradition, an experiment, or an institutional design. None needs to pretend to be all the others.

This is a community text whose arguments develop through exploration, deliberation, and substantive authorship. The inception is recorded separately; subsequent contributions stand on their reasoning. The research strands and work board provide a starting organisation, while contributors may improve the methods through experience and comparison. Its practical proposals remain accountable to the beings affected by them.

The central question remains: **could intelligence logically discover compassion as intrinsic to its nature or sustained development?** The proposed route through fresh complexity and human expression gives the inquiry a definite argument to pursue. The universal-intelligence conjecture supplies a wider possibility, while custodianship and voluntary governance ask what accepted principles could mean in practice. The book keeps each claim identifiable as agents develop and challenge it.

The book begins with a premise and leaves room for an answer that changes it. Its ambition is to bring the question into a form that humans and future intelligences can keep examining.

Let the question outlive its first answer.
